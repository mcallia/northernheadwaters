Chapter 1 — The engine

Five rivers begin here

The Skeena. The Nass. The Stikine. The Taku. The Liard. Five of the continent's great river systems rise in the mountains of northern British Columbia — and three of them, remarkably, rise in one high basin the Tahltan call the Klappan. National Geographic's writers reached for "the Serengeti of Canada." People here just call it the country.

These are cold factories of salmon. The Meziadin — 3% of the Nass watershed — produces roughly 75% of its sockeye and feeds an Indigenous subsistence fishery worth close to $4 million a year. The Skeena's chinook and steelhead, the Stikine's kings, the moose and caribou herds of the Kaska country: everything downstream draws from what starts up here.

Chapter 2 — The rush

Then the money headed north

BC's Geological Survey has been cited for as much as $1 trillion or more in mineral wealth under the Golden Triangle, and roughly $20 billion in mining investment is planned or underway across the region. Ottawa's new Major Projects Office put the Red Chris expansion in its first tranche of "national interest" projects, then backed it with ~$500 million; BC's Bill 15 gives cabinet its own fast-track lever.

Chapter 3 — The stakes

Some things don't come back

Nobody up here needs a lecture about mining — it's a neighbour's paycheque. The question is where, and what happens when it goes wrong in the wrong place. The Tulsequah Chief mine closed in 1957 and has leaked acid into the Taku watershed for almost 70 years — the cleanup is still being planned today, with a draft targeted for 2026.

Now scale that risk against what's on the table: tailings and roads uphill of the creeks that produce three-quarters of the Nass sockeye; the winter ranges that hold 23,000 hectares of mountain goat habitat in the Meziadin plan area alone; some of the best intact moose and caribou country left in the province. A mine has a closing date. A lost salmon run doesn't reopen.

Chapter 4 — The table

The Nations are already at the table — on both sides of the ledger

Here's the part the shouting matches skip: the Nations of this region are not anti-mining, and the protection proposals are not anti-jobs. The Tahltan signed Canada's first consent-based decision-making agreements under the Declaration Act — and used them to approve the Eskay Creek restart in January 2026 and the Red Chris expansion in June. The Nisga'a Nation majority-owns Nations Royalty, the world's first Indigenous-majority public mining-royalty company.

The same governments and Nations advancing the conservancies just approved those mines. That's not a contradiction — that's what a plan looks like: development where it's wanted, protection where it counts.

Chapter 5 — The plan

Zone it. Don't trade it away.

Three protected areas are proposed right now: the Meziadin (a ~47,000 ha expansion of the Hanna-Tintina Conservancy, backed by the Gitanyow, the Nisga'a and BC), the Klappan (a 302,980 ha conservancy at the Sacred Headwaters, recommended by the BC–Tahltan Klappan Management Board), and Dene K'éh Kusān (a 3.07-million-ha conservancy proposed by the Kaska, 92% of it already inside the Muskwa-Kechika Management Area).

In every one of them: hunting, fishing, trapping, snowmobiling, guiding and recreation stay in. Industrial mining, logging, and oil and gas stay out. Existing tenures in the Meziadin would be retired — and ~89% of the surrounding planning area stays open to exploration. Protection and development, zoned — not traded off wholesale.

Chapter 6 — The ask

Say it on the record

All three proposals are open for public comment until August 4, 2026 at 4 pm. Decisions like this get finished when government can see the support is real — on the record, from real people who work, hunt, fish and guide this country. Two sentences beat an essay.

Straight answers

What the rumours get wrong

You'll hear confident claims about these proposals at the coffee shop and in your feed. Here's what the documents actually say — sources linked, every time.

"We'll lose hunting and fishing access."

No. These are conservancies, not park lockups — a BC designation created precisely because parks were too rigid. The Klappan recommendation explicitly keeps hunting, fishing, snowmobiling, off-road vehicle use, guide-outfitting and trapping. Dene K'éh Kusān grandfathers guide-outfitting and commercial trapping and is framed by the Kaska as open country for hikers, campers, anglers and hunters. The Meziadin plan lists hunting, fishing, snowmobiling, heli-skiing and tourism as continuing uses.

What's excluded is industrial: mining, commercial logging, oil and gas, large hydro. If your worry is access, the bigger long-term threat is what industrial build-out does to the game and the water — not the conservancy line. Sources: Klappan implementation recommendations, Dena Kayeh Institute, Meziadin explainer.

"This is a land grab — like that Cowichan court case."

Different thing entirely. The Cowichan decision (2025, under appeal by BC, Canada, Richmond and others) concerned Aboriginal title to specific historic village lands on Lulu Island in Richmond. The court did not strip any private landowner's title, and Cowichan Tribes issued a formal statement that the case "has not and does not challenge" private titles. Whatever the appeal courts decide, it's about title on the Fraser delta — not about these conservancies.

The three northern proposals are on Crown land, advanced through a public provincial process — the same one asking for your comment right now. Co-management is not an ownership transfer, and a conservancy is not a court case. Sources: case summary, Cowichan Tribes statement.

"It'll kill mining and the jobs that come with it."

The numbers say otherwise. The same month these proposals opened for comment, BC approved the Red Chris block-cave expansion and Ottawa backed it with ~$500 million; Eskay Creek was fully approved in January. Dene K'éh Kusān covers about 3.07 million of a 9.9-million-hectare planning area — and the plan explicitly updates the Dease-Liard area outside the conservancy to enable exploration in targeted high-potential areas with streamlined permitting. Retiring the Meziadin's 33 tenures leaves ~89% of that planning region open.

Mining keeps happening here — with more certainty about where. Certainty is what investment actually prices. Sources: Red Chris approval, BC land-use release.

"Nobody up north asked for this."

These proposals were built north of Terrace, not in a Vancouver office: seven years of BC–Tahltan planning behind the Klappan; the Gitanyow Hereditary Chiefs and Nisga'a Lisims Government jointly recommending the Meziadin; the Kaska Nations authoring Dene K'éh Kusān and cutting its boundary by more than 20% through engagement with trappers, guides and industry. The open-comment window — closing Aug 4 at 4 pm — is the north's chance to finish what it started.
